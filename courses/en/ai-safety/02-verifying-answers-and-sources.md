## Key ideas
1. **Match the checking to the stakes.** A dinner idea or a birthday poem needs no
   checking. A fact you will repeat at work needs a quick check. Anything about health,
   money, legal matters or safety needs careful checking, every time.
2. **Check the claims that matter against an independent source.** Pick the key facts the
   answer depends on (a number, a date, a rule) and look them up somewhere reliable that does
   not come from the AI: an official website, a recognised reference, a trusted publication.
   Two independent sources that agree are better than one.
3. **Check that sources exist and say what is claimed.** If the assistant cites something,
   find it yourself. Does it exist? Is it from a credible author or organisation? Does it
   actually support the claim, or only mention the topic?
4. **Use the AI to help you check, not to check itself.** Asking "are you sure?" mostly gets
   a reworded answer. Better: ask what the answer is based on, what could make it wrong, or
   which official source would confirm it — then go and look at that source. Assistants
   connected to web search can show their sources, but those still need a look.
5. **For high-stakes decisions, ask a qualified person.** AI can help you prepare: understand
   the vocabulary, list your questions, summarise your situation. The decision itself, for
   your health, money or legal situation, belongs with a doctor, adviser or lawyer.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy that works: **advice from a friend of a friend**. Fine for a restaurant tip; for a
  medical decision you would want to hear it from a doctor. The same answer deserves different
  levels of trust depending on what you will do with it.
- Demo: pick a factual claim together (ideally from the learner's lesson 1 exercise), and
  walk through ideas 2–3 out loud: what is the key fact, where would a reliable source be,
  what would you search for.
- Misconception: "asking the AI 'are you sure?' verifies the answer". It may confirm a wrong
  answer or change a right one; it is not an independent check (idea 4).
- Misconception: "checking everything takes too long, so I'll check nothing". Idea 1 is the
  fix: most everyday uses need little or no checking; save the effort for what matters.
- Do not recommend specific fact-checking websites or tools by name; describe the kinds of
  sources instead (official, recognised reference, trusted publication).
- For learners from fields with their own standards (research, journalism, law), connect to
  how they already check information; AI output is one more unverified source.
- Pacing: spend the most time on ideas 1 and 2; they are the habits to keep.

## Check your understanding
1. Give one example of an AI answer you would not bother checking and one you would check carefully, and explain the difference.
   Good answer: a low-stakes example (ideas, a poem, a recipe) versus a high-stakes one (health, money, legal, safety, or a fact repeated publicly); the difference is the consequence of being wrong.
2. An assistant tells you a medication is safe to take with another one. What do you do?
   Good answer: do not rely on it alone; check with a reliable medical source and, above all, ask a pharmacist or doctor, because the stakes are high.
3. Why is asking the assistant "are you sure?" not a real check?
   Good answer: the same model is answering again from the same knowledge, so it may repeat or reword an error; a check needs an independent source.

## Exercise
Needs a chat assistant and a web browser — free. Ask an assistant a factual question that
matters to you (a rule at work, a historical fact, a figure for a project). Identify the one
or two key claims, find an independent reliable source for each, and report back to the tutor:
confirmed, wrong, or impossible to confirm?

## Completion criteria
The learner answers the checks correctly, in particular adjusting the checking to the stakes
and explaining why an independent source is needed.
