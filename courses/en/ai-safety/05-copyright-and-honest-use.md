## Key ideas
1. **AI learns from other people's work.** Models are trained on huge amounts of text, images
   and code created by people. Most of the time they produce something new, but they can
   sometimes reproduce or closely imitate an existing work, a recognisable style or a
   protected character.
2. **The rules are not settled and differ between places.** Questions like "can a model be
   trained on this work?" or "who owns what an AI produced?" are answered differently in
   different countries, and are still being decided by courts and lawmakers. This lesson
   does not give legal answers; for anything important, check the rules where you are or ask
   a qualified person.
3. **Respect other people's work.** Do not ask an AI to copy a book, article, song or artwork,
   or to pass off a copy as your own. Be extra careful with content you will publish or sell,
   and check that it is not a near-copy of something that exists.
4. **Be honest about using AI when it matters.** At school, at work and when publishing,
   people often expect to know whether something was written or made with AI. Follow your
   school's, employer's or publisher's rules, and when there are none, ask yourself: would
   the reader feel misled if they knew?
5. **You stay responsible for what you use.** If you send, submit or publish AI-made content,
   it is yours: its errors (lessons 1–2), its biases (lesson 4) and its originality. Review
   it as if you wrote it, because in the eyes of others you did.

## Teaching notes
- **Do not state any law, court decision or rule as fact.** Laws on copyright and AI differ by
  country and change quickly. Keep to the general picture in idea 2, and if the learner asks
  a specific legal question, say you cannot give legal advice and suggest checking official
  sources or a qualified person in their country.
- Analogy that works: **a group project**. Using a friend's help is fine; handing in the
  friend's work under your name is not. AI help is similar: the question is how you use it
  and whether you are honest about it.
- Misconception: "if an AI made it, it's free of copyright, so I can do anything with it".
  Not necessarily: the output may resemble protected work, and ownership rules differ.
- Misconception: "using AI is always cheating". It depends on the context and the rules;
  many schools and workplaces allow it with disclosure. The issue is honesty and the rules,
  not the tool itself.
- Close the course: connect the five lessons as one habit — use AI, but check what matters,
  protect what is private, notice bias, and be honest about your use.
- For experienced learners, discuss a simple disclosure line they could use at work
  ("Drafted with an AI assistant, reviewed and edited by me").

## Check your understanding
1. Why can AI-made content raise copyright concerns?
   Good answer: models learn from people's work and can sometimes reproduce or closely imitate existing works or styles; the rules on training and ownership are unsettled and differ by country.
2. A student uses an AI assistant to help write an essay. What should they do to use it honestly?
   Good answer: check and follow the school's or teacher's rules on AI, disclose the use where expected, and make sure the work they submit reflects their own understanding.
3. You publish a blog post drafted with AI and it contains a wrong fact. Who is responsible?
   Good answer: the person who published it; using AI does not transfer responsibility, so content must be reviewed before it is sent or published.

## Exercise
Needs nothing (or a chat assistant) — free. Think of one place where you use or could use AI
(school, work, a hobby, social media). Find out or decide what the rules are there, and write
a one-sentence disclosure you would be comfortable adding to AI-assisted work. Discuss with
the tutor when you would use it.

## Completion criteria
The learner answers the checks correctly, in particular that they remain responsible for
AI-made content they use, and that disclosure depends on the context and its rules.
