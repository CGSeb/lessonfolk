## Key ideas
1. **If "good" is not written down, everyone judges differently.** Two people reading the same
   answer can disagree because one cares about accuracy and the other about tone. Even
   you, on two different days, may judge differently. Writing down what good means is the
   first step of any evaluation.
2. **Break "good" into a few dimensions.** Common ones are: *correct* (facts and reasoning are
   right), *complete* (nothing important is missing), *on task* (follows the instructions and
   stays on topic), *appropriate* (tone and level fit the reader), *well formed* (the
   requested format), and *safe* (no harmful or private content). Different tasks weigh them
   differently: a legal summary needs correctness first, a greeting card needs tone.
3. **Make each criterion observable.** "Clear" is a feeling; "states the action to take in the
   first sentence, uses no jargon, stays under 100 words" can be checked by anyone. When you
   cannot say how you would tell, the criterion is still too vague.
4. **Separate must-pass from nice-to-have.** Some requirements are not a matter of degree: no
   invented facts, no personal data in the answer, the right refusal. Failing one makes the
   answer fail, however well written. Other qualities (concise, friendly) are scored on a
   scale, and a weakness there is a cost, not a disaster.
5. **Keep the rubric short and let it evolve.** Three to five criteria are enough. Short
   examples of a good answer and a poor one help people apply them the same way. When you
   see an answer that is clearly bad but passes, or good but fails, the rubric is missing
   something: improve it.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy: a teacher marking essays without a marking scheme. Two markers, two grades. The
  scheme does not make the marking mechanical, it makes it comparable.
- Scenario to reason through with the learner (nothing to produce): a team wants an AI to
  summarise customer emails and says "the summary should be good". Ask what they could mean
  by good, which two criteria they would write first, and which one could never be traded away.
- Misconception: "criteria kill nuance". They give the nuance a shared name, so that it can be
  discussed and improved.
- Misconception: "more criteria are better". A long rubric is slow, and people stop applying it
  carefully; keep it short.
- Beginners to the course: use the dimensions list as a menu and stay with the email-summary
  scenario. Experienced learners: discuss weighting and how a must-pass rule changes the score.
- Do not tie the rubric to a particular tool or score scale; any simple scale works.
- Pacing: idea 3 is where learners struggle most; spend time on rewriting vague words.

## Check your understanding
1. Why should you decide what "good" means before looking at the answers?
   Good answer: otherwise each judge, or the same judge on another day, uses a different
   standard, and the results cannot be compared; criteria written first keep the judging fair.
2. A team's criterion is "the reply should be friendly and professional". Rewrite it so that
   someone else could check it.
   Good answer: turn it into observable points, for example greets by name, no slang, no
   blame, ends with a clear next step; the exact points matter less than being checkable.
3. For an assistant that summarises customer emails, give one must-pass requirement and one
   nice-to-have quality, and say how a failure would be treated in each case.
   Good answer: must-pass such as "no invented details" or "no customer data revealed" (a
   failure makes the summary fail); nice-to-have such as "short" (a weakness lowers the score).

## Completion criteria
The learner can explain why criteria come first, turn a vague quality into checkable points and
distinguish must-pass from scored qualities (checks 1-3).
