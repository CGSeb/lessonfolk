## Key ideas
1. **Start with a map of the topic.** When a subject is new, ask for an overview: the main
   ideas, the key words to know, the usual questions people ask, and what to learn first. It
   is like asking a knowledgeable friend where to begin.
2. **Ask for explanations at your level, then go deeper.** Say what you already know ("I'm
   new to this", "I know the basics of accounting") and ask for examples from your own life
   or work. When something is unclear, ask again differently: an analogy, a simpler version,
   a concrete case.
3. **Use AI to test yourself.** Ask it to quiz you, to check your own explanation of the
   topic, or to point out what you got wrong. Explaining something back is one of the
   best ways to learn it.
4. **Use it to explore, not as the final source.** AI is a fast way to find what to look for
   and to understand it. For facts you will rely on or repeat, check them in reliable sources
   (covered in Using AI Safely and Wisely). Asking for the key terms to search for makes that
   checking easier.
5. **Know what it may not know.** Very recent developments, local details or very specialised
   knowledge may be missing or wrong. Assistants with web search can help with recent topics,
   but their sources still need a look.

## Teaching notes
- You are doing idea 2 and 3 right now as a tutor: point that out, it makes the lesson
  concrete ("this course itself is AI-assisted learning").
- Analogy that works: **a patient study partner** who has read a lot but sometimes
  misremembers. Great for explaining and quizzing; not the last word on facts.
- Demo: let the learner pick a topic they are curious about. Ask for a map (idea 1), then
  explain one point at their level, then quiz them with one question.
- Misconception: "if I understood the AI's explanation, I learned it". Understanding while
  reading is not the same as being able to explain or apply it; idea 3 closes that gap.
- Misconception: "AI research replaces reading sources". It speeds up finding and
  understanding sources; it does not replace checking them. Keep the verification reminder to
  one or two sentences and point to the safety course rather than re-teaching it.
- For learners in research-heavy jobs, focus on idea 4: using AI to find search terms,
  structure a question and spot what they do not know yet.
- Stay tool-neutral; do not name search or learning products.

## Check your understanding
1. You need to understand a new topic at work quickly. What would your first request to an assistant look like?
   Good answer: asks for an overview or map of the topic (main ideas, key terms, where to start), says their current level and why they need it.
2. How can an AI assistant help you check that you really understood something?
   Good answer: by quizzing them, or by checking and correcting their own explanation of the topic.
3. When is it not enough to rely on what an assistant told you while researching?
   Good answer: for facts they will rely on, repeat or decide with, and for recent, local or very specialised information; those need checking in reliable sources.

## Exercise
Needs your own chat assistant — free (a web browser helps for checking). Pick a topic you
want to learn for work or a hobby. Ask for a map of it, ask for an explanation of one part at
your level, then ask to be quizzed with three questions. Finally, check one key fact in a
reliable source and tell the tutor what you found.

## Completion criteria
The learner answers the checks correctly and can describe a research approach that uses AI to
explore and to self-test, plus checking for key facts.
