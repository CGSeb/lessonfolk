## Key ideas
1. **Go wide first, then narrow.** Ask for many options at once ("20 ideas for…"), including
   unusual ones. Most will be ordinary; a few will be useful or will spark your own idea.
   Then ask it to group them, or pick the best ones against what matters to you.
2. **Ask it to argue against you.** Assistants tend to agree with the person they are
   talking to. Turn that around: "Play devil's advocate: what are the strongest arguments
   against my plan?" or "What would a sceptical customer say?"
3. **Compare choices with your own criteria.** List what matters (cost, time, risk, what you
   enjoy) and ask for a comparison table of your options against those criteria. Writing the
   criteria down is often half the decision.
4. **Look for what you missed.** Ask "What am I not considering?" or "What could go wrong,
   and how would I notice early?" AI is good at listing angles you did not think of.
5. **The decision stays yours.** The assistant does not know your full situation, values or
   constraints, and it can be wrong. Use it to think more clearly, not to decide for you,
   especially for decisions that affect other people.

## Teaching notes
- Link to Prompting Basics lesson 4 (leading questions) and to bias in Using AI Safely and
  Wisely: idea 2 is the practical fix for an assistant's tendency to agree.
- Analogy that works: **a whiteboard session with a colleague who never gets tired**. They
  throw out lots of ideas and will happily argue the other side, but you are the one who
  knows the situation and makes the call.
- Demo: take a small real decision from the learner (a weekend plan, a gift, a work choice),
  generate options, then challenge their favourite and make a quick criteria table.
- Misconception: "the AI's top option is the best one". Its ranking depends on what it
  assumed you care about; give it your criteria (idea 3).
- Misconception: "asking AI is neutral advice". It may lean toward your framing or toward
  common, average choices; ask for the opposite view.
- For big personal decisions (career, health, money), be supportive but clear: AI helps
  structure the thinking; people and qualified professionals help with the decision.

## Check your understanding
1. Why is it useful to ask an assistant to argue against your idea?
   Good answer: assistants tend to agree with the user, so asking for counter-arguments gets a more honest view and reveals weaknesses.
2. You have three job offers. How could an assistant help without deciding for you?
   Good answer: help list their own criteria, build a comparison table, point out what they may be missing and questions to ask, while they make the decision.
3. What is a good way to start a brainstorm with AI?
   Good answer: ask for many ideas at once, including unusual ones, then narrow down or group them against what matters to them.

## Exercise
Needs your own chat assistant — free. Pick a real, low-stakes choice you are facing. Ask for
at least 10 options, choose your favourite, ask the assistant to argue against it, and build a
comparison table with your own criteria. Tell the tutor whether your choice changed.
