## Key ideas
1. **A good summary has a purpose and a reader.** "Summarise this" gets a generic shortening.
   "Summarise this meeting for my team: the 3 decisions and who does what by when" gets
   something you can use. Say what the summary is for, who will read it and how long it
   should be.
2. **Ask for what you would look for yourself.** Decisions, deadlines, risks, open questions,
   the one number that matters: name them, and the assistant will pull them out instead of
   retelling everything in order.
3. **A draft is a starting point, not a finished text.** Assistants are good at getting past
   the blank page: a first version of an email, a report section, a post. Give the key
   points you want to make, the reader and the tone, then edit.
4. **Make it yours.** Change the wording to sound like you, cut what you would never say, and
   check every fact, name, date and number in it. Generic AI phrasing is easy to spot, and the
   text goes out under your name.
5. **Summaries can miss or twist things.** A summary can leave out a key point or state it
   more strongly than the original did. For anything important, skim the original for the
   parts the summary relies on.

## Teaching notes
- Link to Prompting Basics: this is lesson 1 (context, goal, audience) applied to two everyday
  tasks. One sentence of recap is enough.
- Analogy that works: **an assistant who takes notes for you**. Tell them what you care about
  before the meeting, and the notes are useful; say nothing, and you get a transcript.
- Demo: take a short text the learner provides (or a paragraph you write together) and
  summarise it twice: once with "summarise this", once with a purpose, reader and length.
  Compare.
- Misconception: "the summary covers everything important". It covers what looks important
  from the text, not what is important to you (idea 2), and it can drop nuances (idea 5).
- Misconception: "sending the AI draft as-is saves time". Unedited drafts often sound generic
  and can contain errors; the time saved comes from not starting from zero, not from skipping
  the review.
- Remind briefly (one sentence) not to paste confidential text, linking to the privacy lesson
  of Using AI Safely and Wisely.
- Stay tool-neutral: do not name products or features.

## Check your understanding
1. Rewrite "Summarise this report" so the summary is actually useful for a specific reader.
   Good answer: adds the purpose, the reader and a length or format, and ideally what to look for (e.g. "for my manager, in 5 bullet points: the main findings, the costs and the decision needed").
2. What should you do with an AI draft before sending it?
   Good answer: edit it to sound like you, remove what does not fit, and check facts, names, dates and numbers, because they are responsible for what they send.
3. Why should you still glance at the original for an important summary?
   Good answer: a summary can leave out key points or change their strength or meaning, so the parts that matter need checking against the source.

## Exercise
Needs your own chat assistant — free. Take a real text from your week (a long email thread,
an article, meeting notes without confidential details). Ask for a summary with a purpose,
reader and length. Then ask for a short reply or follow-up draft, and edit it until you would
send it. Tell the tutor what you changed and why.

## Completion criteria
The learner answers the checks correctly and writes at least one summary request that states
a purpose and a reader.
