## Key ideas
1. **The model copies the data, flaws included.** A fine-tuned model learns whatever the
   examples have in common: their good style, but also their errors, their inconsistencies,
   their outdated answers and their biases. A smaller set of clean, consistent examples
   usually beats a large pile of mixed quality. Start small, measure, and add data where the
   test set shows weakness.
2. **Cover the real cases, including the hard ones.** The examples should look like what the
   model will meet: the same mix of requests, the long and the short, the angry and the
   unclear. Include cases where the right answer is to ask a question, to say "I do not know"
   or to pass the case to a person. If every training example has a confident answer, the
   model learns to always give one.
3. **Keep the test set apart, and watch for leaks.** Set aside examples that are never used in
   training, and score only on them. If the same or nearly the same case appears in both
   (a ticket and its duplicate, two replies to one thread), the score is inflated: the model is
   remembering, not generalising. Check for near-duplicates across the split.
4. **Models can repeat their training data.** A model can memorise and later reproduce pieces
   of what it was trained on, sometimes word for word. Remove personal data, secrets and
   confidential details you would not want any user to see, check that you are allowed to use
   the data for training, and remember that a trained model cannot simply forget one record.
5. **Synthetic data helps, with conditions.** Examples generated by a larger model can fill gaps
   (rare cases, other languages) quickly. But they carry that model's errors and habits, so they
   need review, and they tend to be less varied than real cases. Check the generating model's
   terms of use too: some forbid using its outputs to train other models.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy that works: **an apprentice who copies the master's notebook**. If the notebook mixes
  three people's handwriting, old prices and a few mistakes, the apprentice learns all of it,
  faithfully.
- Running case: the support team proposes to train on five years of replies written by their
  agents. Ask the learner what could go wrong before revealing it: three different house styles
  over the years, outdated product answers, replies with customers' names and order details, a
  lot of near-duplicate replies to the same common question, and few examples of escalation.
- Misconception: "more data is always better". More inconsistent data teaches inconsistency.
- Misconception: "the test score is high, so it works". Ask whether the test set was truly kept
  apart and checked for near-duplicates.
- Misconception: "we can delete a customer's data from the model later". Removing specific
  information from a trained model is not a simple operation; keep it out in the first place.
- Do not quote minimum dataset sizes; they depend on the task and the method, and the advice
  "start small and measure" holds.
- Do not give legal advice on data use; say that consent, contracts and data protection rules
  apply and should be checked.
- Pacing: the five-year dataset scenario carries the lesson; let the learner find most of the
  problems themselves.

## Check your understanding
1. The support team wants to train on five years of replies written by their agents. Name three risks in this dataset.
   Good answer: three of: inconsistent styles over the years, outdated or wrong product answers, personal data such as names and order details, near-duplicates that will leak between training and test, too few hard cases such as escalations or unclear requests.
2. Why must the test set be kept separate, and what happens if near-duplicate tickets end up in both sets?
   Good answer: the test must measure how the model does on cases it has not seen; near-duplicates let it score by remembering, so the score is inflated and the real-world quality is lower than measured.
3. Why is it risky to leave customers' names and addresses in the training data, even if the model is only used internally?
   Good answer: models can memorise and reproduce training data, so personal data may come out in answers to other users; it cannot simply be removed from the trained model later; it may also break data protection rules or contracts.
4. Why should a training set include examples where the right answer is to escalate or to say "I do not know"?
   Good answer: the model learns the pattern of its examples; if every example has a confident answer, it learns to always answer confidently, even when it should pass the case on or admit uncertainty.
