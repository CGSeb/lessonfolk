## Key ideas
1. **A job is a bundle of tasks, not a single thing.** A receptionist answers calls, greets
   visitors, books rooms, sorts post and calms upset people. AI does not arrive "for the job":
   it arrives for particular tasks, and it is much better at some than at others.
2. **For each task, AI can replace or assist.** *Replacing* means the task is done without
   a person (a form is sorted automatically). *Assisting* means a person does it faster or
   better with help (a draft reply that they review and send). Most workplace uses today are
   assisting, and the person stays responsible for the result.
3. **When some tasks shrink, the job usually shifts.** If drafting takes ten minutes instead
   of an hour, the person has time for the tasks left: talking to customers, checking, deciding.
   A well-known example is the cash machine: in the decades after it spread, the number of bank
   tellers in the United States kept growing for a long time, as their work moved from handling
   cash toward helping customers. Automation can also shrink jobs, and one example does not
   prove what will happen with AI, but it shows that "the task is automated" and "the job
   disappears" are different things.
4. **Some tasks are more exposed than others.** Tasks done on a computer, built from text or
   images, repeated often and easy to check are the most exposed: summarising, drafting,
   translating, sorting, routine code. Tasks in unpredictable physical settings, built on
   human trust and contact, or where someone must be accountable, are less exposed.
   "Less exposed" is not "safe": what AI can do keeps changing.
5. **Be sceptical of big predictions.** "AI will replace X% of jobs" is usually a guess
   built on assumptions, and it often measures how many tasks *could* be done by AI, not how
   many jobs *will* disappear. Real outcomes depend on cost, quality, law, and the choices
   of employers and workers. Ask who is making the claim, what exactly it measures and over what
   time.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy: a job is a toolbox, and AI is a new tool that fits some compartments. The whole
  box is rarely emptied at once, but what you reach for most often can change.
- Scenario to reason through with the learner (no task to do): take a job they know (a nurse,
  an accountant, a teacher) and walk through five or six of its tasks one by one. For each, ask
  whether AI could replace it, help with it, or not really, and why. Making the idea concrete
  on a real job is the most useful moment of the lesson.
- Misconception: "AI takes jobs" or "AI changes nothing". Both are too simple. Keep the
  discussion balanced and avoid either alarm or reassurance; the honest answer is "it
  depends, and it is uneven".
- Be respectful: a learner may be worried about their own work. Acknowledge the worry
  without promising either outcome.
- Do not quote statistics, named studies or dates in the lesson: figures vary between
  sources and age quickly. If the learner raises a figure, use idea 5 to examine it.
- Beginners: stay with ideas 1, 2 and 5 and the task-list exercise. Experienced learners can
  go deeper on exposure and on how new tasks (reviewing AI output) appear.
- Pacing: leave about half the time for the task-list exercise.

## Check your understanding
1. In your own words, why is it more useful to ask "which tasks of my job will change?" than
   "will AI take my job?"
   Good answer: a job is made of several tasks and AI affects them unevenly, so looking at
   tasks shows what can be replaced, what can be helped and what stays human.
2. Give one example of AI replacing a task and one of AI assisting a person with a task.
   Good answer: for example automatic sorting of messages (replacing) and a drafted reply
   the person reviews and sends (assisting); any clear pair works.
3. You read: "AI will wipe out 40% of jobs within five years." What questions would you ask
   before believing or sharing it?
   Good answer: who says it and why, what it measures (tasks AI could do or jobs actually
   lost), what the assumptions and the time frame are, and what the evidence is.
4. Pick three tasks from a job you know. Say which AI could replace, which it could assist
   with, and which it could hardly touch, and why.
   Good answer: a reasoned sorting that uses ideas 2 and 4 (digital, repeatable, checkable
   tasks more exposed; contact, trust and accountability less), with reasons rather than guesses.

## Completion criteria
The learner can describe a job as tasks, distinguish replacing from assisting, apply both to a
real example, and question a headline prediction (checks 1-4).
