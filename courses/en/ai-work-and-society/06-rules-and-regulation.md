## Key ideas
1. **There is no single worldwide rulebook.** Countries and regions make their own laws on AI,
   and they differ in how strict they are and in what they focus on. Many rules are still
   being written or changed, so what is true today may not be next year.
2. **A common idea is to regulate by risk.** The more an AI system can affect people's rights,
   safety or opportunities (for example in hiring, lending, health or policing), the stricter
   the rules: testing, documentation, human oversight. Low-risk uses face few rules, and a few
   uses can be banned. The European Union's AI Act is a well-known example of this approach.
3. **Transparency duties are common.** Rules often ask that people be told when they are
   talking to a machine or looking at AI-made content, and that some AI-made media be labelled.
   The details vary by place.
4. **Existing laws already apply.** Privacy, consumer protection, anti-discrimination,
   copyright, defamation and fraud law do not disappear because AI was involved. "The AI did
   it" is not a defence.
5. **Responsibility sits with people and organisations.** When AI causes harm, the person or
   company that built, sold or used it is generally the one asked to answer for it, not the
   software. For you, the rules that count most day to day are often closer to home: your
   employer's AI policy, your school's rules, and the terms of the services you use. Read them.
   You also have a voice: follow reliable news on the topic and use complaint or feedback routes.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy: cars are regulated by risk and use: a bicycle and a lorry do not face the same rules,
  and everyone is still bound by general laws such as those on dangerous driving.
- Exercise: ask the learner to pick an AI use they know (CV screening, a school writing tool, a
  chatbot on a shop site) and place it as low, medium or high risk with a reason.
- Misconception: "AI is unregulated". Many general laws already apply, and AI-specific ones exist
  in some places. The picture is patchy, not empty.
- Misconception: "regulation just blocks innovation" or "it will fix everything". Present the
  trade-offs evenly; this is a topic where people reasonably disagree.
- This is not legal advice: say so, and encourage the learner to check the rules where they
  live or ask a qualified person for anything important.
- Stay neutral on politics and on current bills. Do not give dates, article numbers, thresholds or
  fines, and do not claim how any law is enforced in a specific country: these change often.
- Beginners: ideas 1, 4 and 5. Experienced learners: discuss risk tiers and transparency duties.
- Pacing: end with the exercise, it makes the principles concrete.

## Check your understanding
1. Why can't you assume the same AI rules apply in every country?
   Good answer: each country or region makes its own laws, which differ and keep changing, so
   the rules depend on where you and the service are.
2. In your own words, what does "regulating by risk" mean? Give an example of a high-risk use.
   Good answer: stricter rules where AI can seriously affect people (such as hiring, loans,
   health, policing) and lighter ones for low-risk uses.
3. An employee sends a customer an AI-written reply with a wrong refund promise. Who is
   responsible, and what rule near the employee might matter?
   Good answer: the person and company using the tool, not "the AI"; the employer's AI policy
   and general consumer rules matter, which is why people review AI output before sending.

## Completion criteria
The learner can explain why rules differ, describe regulation by risk, say that responsibility
rests with people and organisations, and name the rules closest to their daily use (checks 1-3).
