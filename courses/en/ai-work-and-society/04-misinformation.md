## Key ideas
1. **Two different problems.** *Misinformation* is false content shared by mistake;
   *disinformation* is false content made or spread on purpose. AI can add to both: a
   chatbot that is confidently wrong (covered in *Using AI Safely and Wisely*) is an accident;
   an operation that uses AI to flood the web with fakes is deliberate.
2. **AI lowers the cost, so the volume goes up.** Convincing articles, fake reviews, invented
   quotes, realistic images and many fake accounts can be produced in minutes. The change is
   less about better lies than about many more of them, tailored to different audiences.
3. **A second effect: real things can be dismissed as fake.** When anything could be forged,
   a person caught on a real recording can say "it's AI". Sometimes the damage of fakes is that
   we trust less, including what is true.
4. **Detectors and labels are not a verdict.** Tools that claim to spot AI-made text or images
   make mistakes both ways, accusing real work and missing fakes. Watermarks and content
   credentials can help when they are present, but they can be missing or removed, so the
   absence of a label proves nothing.
5. **Check the source, not the look.** Pause on content that makes you angry or afraid. Ask who
   published it, then leave the page and see what others say about that source (reading
   "laterally"). Look for the original: the full video, the first publication, a reverse image
   search. For breaking news, wait for reliable outlets to confirm. And do not share what you
   have not checked.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy: printing used to be expensive, so most printed things had a publisher behind them.
  Now everyone can produce a polished page, so the question moves from "does it look
  professional?" to "who is behind it and can it be confirmed?".
- Scenario to reason through with the learner (no task to do): a friend shares an invented claim
  ("a town banned all cars after a new study") and wants to repost it. Ask what would make
  you doubt it and what checks would settle it before sharing.
- Misconception: "I can tell AI images by the hands or the shine". Such tells change as tools
  improve; treat them as weak hints at best.
- Misconception: "an AI detector will tell me". Present idea 4 clearly; false accusations
  against students and writers are a real problem.
- Stay non-partisan: use neutral examples and avoid current political events.
- Connect to the earlier course: verification habits for chatbot answers (*Using AI Safely and
  Wisely*) apply here to content from anywhere.
- Do not cite figures on how much content online is AI-made: they are uncertain and age fast.
- Beginners: ideas 1, 4 and 5. Experienced learners: discuss platform responsibility and
  provenance standards.

## Check your understanding
1. What is the difference between misinformation and disinformation, and how does AI affect each?
   Good answer: misinformation is false content shared by mistake, disinformation is made or
   spread on purpose; AI adds both through confident errors and through cheap mass-produced fakes.
2. A friend says: "The detector said 98% AI, so this essay is definitely fake." What would you reply?
   Good answer: detectors make errors in both directions and are not proof; look at other
   evidence, ask about the process, and avoid accusing on a score alone.
3. A shocking photo is spreading and you are about to share it. List two checks you would make first.
   Good answer: find the original source and the date, search for the image elsewhere, see what
   reliable outlets say, and leave the page to check who is behind the account.

## Completion criteria
The learner can explain how AI raises the volume of false content, why detectors and labels do
not settle the question, and apply source-checking habits to a case (checks 1-3).
