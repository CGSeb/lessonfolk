## Key ideas
1. **When routine tasks get cheaper, judgement gets more valuable.** If a draft, a summary or
   a first version costs almost nothing, the scarce part is deciding what is good, what is
   true, what is right for this person and this situation. Judgement, knowing your field
   and its context, and being able to spot a flaw are the skills to protect.
2. **People skills stay central.** Building trust, listening, negotiating, teaching, caring,
   and being someone others can hold accountable are hard to hand to a tool, and often
   matter more when everything else is automated.
3. **Using AI well is a skill too.** Knowing when AI helps, how to ask clearly, and how to
   check what comes back (covered in *Prompting Basics* and *AI for Everyday Work*) makes
   your own work better. Tools change fast, so aim to learn habits (clear goals, checking,
   keeping notes of what works) rather than the buttons of one app.
4. **Don't let the tool do all the thinking.** If you always ask AI for the answer, the skill
   behind it can fade, and you lose the ability to notice when the answer is wrong. While you
   are learning something, try first, then ask AI to critique your attempt, explain, or
   quiz you, rather than to simply do it.
5. **Change is not equally shared, and it is not only your job to adapt.** Access to tools,
   training and time to learn varies between people and workplaces. Ask for training, share
   what you learn with colleagues, and notice who is being left out. Adapting is easier with
   support than alone.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy: a calculator did not remove the need to understand numbers; it moved the
  value to knowing which calculation to run and whether the result makes sense. AI does
  the same for writing, summarising and first drafts.
- Scenario to reason through with the learner (no task to do): someone hands every first draft
  to AI and, a year later, struggles to write one alone. Ask which skill faded, which task
  was fine to hand over, and what that suggests about the skills worth keeping in practice.
- Misconception: "learn the one AI tool and you are set". Tools change quickly; habits last.
- Misconception: "soft skills are safe forever". Do not promise safety; say these skills tend
  to gain value, and that nobody can be sure.
- Be careful not to blame the individual: idea 5 matters, especially for learners worried
  about being "left behind".
- Beginners: ideas 1, 3 and 4 with the exercise. Experienced learners: discuss the balance
  between delegating and keeping the skill.
- Do not name particular tools, courses, job titles in demand, or statistics: they age fast.

## Check your understanding
1. Why can judgement become more valuable when AI produces drafts quickly?
   Good answer: drafts become cheap, so the scarce part is deciding whether something is
   good, true and right for the situation, which still takes a person's knowledge.
2. Give one habit (not a tool) that helps you use AI well, and say why it lasts when tools change.
   Good answer: for example setting a clear goal, checking outputs, or keeping notes on what
   works; habits apply to any tool, while button-by-button knowledge goes out of date.
3. You are learning to write reports and always ask AI to write them for you. What is the risk,
   and what would you do instead?
   Good answer: your own skill may not develop and you may miss weak points in the output;
   write a first attempt yourself, then ask AI for critique, explanations or practice questions.

## Completion criteria
The learner can name skills that tend to gain value, treat using AI well as a habit-based
skill, and describe how to use AI to learn without letting their own skill fade (checks 1-3).
