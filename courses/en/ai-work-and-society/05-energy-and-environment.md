## Key ideas
1. **AI runs in physical places.** Your question travels to a *data centre*, a building full of
   powerful computers. They use electricity to compute and to be cooled. Many designs use
   water for cooling, and producing the electricity can use water too. "The cloud" has a
   footprint.
2. **Two phases use resources.** *Training* a model takes a very large amount of computing, but
   it happens once in a while and few organisations do it. *Using* a model (every answer you
   get) is small each time, but billions of requests add up, and estimates suggest that use
   now accounts for most of AI's computing.
3. **The numbers are uncertain.** The footprint of one answer depends on the model, the
   hardware, the length of the request, where it runs and how the electricity is produced, and
   companies share little detail. Be wary of both extremes: a viral figure ("one question uses a
   bottle of water") may be wrong or lack context, and "it's nothing" ignores the growth in total use.
4. **Where and how matters.** The same computing causes much less carbon where electricity is
   low-carbon than where it comes from burning coal. Water use depends on the climate and the
   cooling method, and a large data centre can strain a local grid or water supply. So the
   impact is both global (emissions) and local (grids, water, communities).
5. **What you can do, without guilt.** Use the right-sized tool for the job (a small model or no
   AI at all for simple tasks), avoid pointless repeated requests, and favour providers that
   share data on energy and use clean electricity. Efficiency keeps improving, but cheaper
   AI can also lead to more use, so total demand matters. Your personal use is a small part;
   rules, company choices and the energy supply matter more.

## Teaching notes
- Analogy: streaming video felt weightless, yet it also relies on data centres. AI is like
  streaming with a much hungrier engine: small per use, large in total.
- Scenario to reason through with the learner (no task to do): two people argue, one saying "AI
  will drain the planet", the other "AI's footprint is nothing". Ask which question each
  one avoids and what a fair statement would look like.
- Misconception: "my one chat is the problem" or "my one chat is nothing". The honest framing is
  that individual use is small, the total is large and growing, and system choices matter most.
- Keep the tone calm and balanced: avoid alarm, avoid dismissal, and respect that
  learners may care a lot about this topic.
- Do not quote per-question figures, total electricity shares or forecasts: estimates differ
  widely between sources and change quickly. If the learner brings one, use idea 3 to examine it.
- Connect to *How AI Models Really Work*, "Choosing a model": a smaller model for simple
  tasks saves cost and energy.
- Beginners: ideas 1, 2 and 3. Experienced learners: discuss rebound effects and transparency.

## Check your understanding
1. Where does an AI answer use electricity and water?
   Good answer: in data centres, where computers run the model and need cooling, which can use
   water, plus the electricity that powers them.
2. What is the difference between the cost of training a model and the cost of using it?
   Good answer: training is a very large, occasional cost done by few organisations; using is
   small per request but repeated billions of times, so it can dominate over time.
3. A post says "each chat message empties a bottle of water". How would you judge it?
   Good answer: ask where the number comes from, what it includes, whether it varies by
   model, location and cooling, and whether it is an estimate; treat precise viral figures with caution.
4. Name one thing you can do to reduce your share of the footprint and one thing that matters more than your own use.
   Good answer: use a smaller tool or none for simple tasks, avoid pointless requests; the
   energy supply, company choices and rules matter more than any single person.

## Completion criteria
The learner can say where AI uses resources, separate training from use, and treat environmental
claims with care while naming sensible actions (checks 1-4).
