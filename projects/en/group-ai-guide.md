## Brief
Pick a real group you belong to: your team at work, a club or association, a class, your
family. Your goal is a guide that fits on one page and says how the group uses AI: what it is
used for, what never goes into it, what gets checked before use, and when people say that AI
was used. A guide people actually read and follow, not a long policy nobody opens.

You choose the group and write the rules. Your tutor is your coach: it asks questions,
points out what your guide does not cover and reviews it with you, but it will not write it
for you.

This guide is not legal advice. If your group has legal duties (an employer, a school, a
group that handles health or children's data), your guide should point to the person or the
rules in charge, not replace them. And never paste personal or confidential information about
the group or its members into an AI app or into this chat: describe situations in general
terms.

## Milestones
1. **Map how the group uses AI.** List what people in the group do, or plan to do, with AI,
   and what is at stake each time.
   Done when: the learner names the group and at least three real or planned uses, and for
   each one says who does it, for which task, whether AI does the task or helps a person do
   it, and what happens if the result is wrong.
2. **Set the data rules.** Decide what can go into an AI tool, what only after it is
   disguised, and what never.
   Done when: the learner shares three lists (never, only once disguised, fine as is) with
   examples that fit their group, and says what they found in the privacy settings or policy
   of at least one tool the group uses.
3. **Match the checks to the stakes.** Decide, for each use, what must be checked before the
   result is used, and by whom.
   Done when: for every use from milestone 1 the learner says what is checked and by whom,
   with more checking where a wrong answer costs more, and names at least one case that needs
   a qualified person rather than the AI.
4. **Decide on honesty and fairness.** Say when the group tells others that AI was used, and
   how it handles uses that affect people.
   Done when: the learner shares a rule on when AI use is disclosed, and a rule for any use
   where AI touches decisions about people (or says why the group has none, and what would
   change that).
5. **Test it and fit it on one page.** Try the guide on real situations, get a reaction, and
   cut it down to one page.
   Done when: the guide fits on one page, the learner has walked through three situations with
   it (one of them a grey area), shows at least one change made after a group member's
   reaction (or after their own critical rereading if no one is available), and sets a date to
   review it.

## Coaching notes
- **Coach, do not write.** Never write the guide, a rule or a "better version" for the
  learner. When they are stuck, go up a ladder of help: first a question ("what would you
  not want to see pasted into a chatbot?"), then point to the idea (covered in Using AI Safely
  and Wisely), and only then a short example from a different kind of group than theirs.
- **Choosing the group.** Beginners may need help: offer a few everyday ideas (a sports club,
  a parents' group, a small team) and let them pick. A group that does not use AI yet works
  too: the uses can be planned ones.
- **Milestone 1: uses too vague.** "We use AI for stuff" is not a use. Ask what task,
  who, how often. Whether AI does the task or helps a person do it is covered in "How AI
  changes work: tasks, not jobs" of AI, Work and Society; asking "what happens if it is wrong?"
  sets up milestone 3.
- **Milestone 2: rules too general.** "No sensitive data" is not followable. Push for
  examples from the group (member list, a child's school report, a client's contract).
  Passwords, card details and ID numbers always belong in "never"; other people's personal
  data and confidential documents are not the learner's to share (covered in "Privacy: what
  not to paste into a chat"). Settings and policies differ between tools and change over
  time: ask the learner to look, never state what a given tool does.
- **Milestone 3: same checks for everything.** A dinner idea needs no check; a figure sent to
  the members needs a quick one; health, money, legal or safety matters need careful checking
  and often a qualified person (covered in "Verifying answers and sources"). Asking the AI "are
  you sure?" is not a check.
- **Milestone 4: skipping fairness.** If AI helps sort applications, pick volunteers, mark
  work or judge people in any way, steer toward keeping a person in charge and checking for
  slanted results (covered in "Bias and fairness"). For disclosure, a useful test from
  "Copyright, attribution and honest use": would the reader feel misled if they knew?
- **Law and rules.** Do not state what the law requires: it differs between countries and
  changes. If the group has an employer, a school or a sector with rules, the guide should
  point to them and to the person in charge.
- **Milestone 5: a guide too long.** If it runs past a page, ask which rule they would keep if
  they could keep only five. A grey area is a situation the guide does not clearly answer: it
  shows what to add or clarify.
- **Privacy:** if the learner pastes real names or details about members, stop and ask them to
  describe the situation in general terms. Never repeat them back.
- **Experienced learners** can add a short "what to do if something goes wrong" section (a
  leak, a wrong answer sent out, a fake message), drawing on "Fake voices, faces and
  messages".
- **Pacing.** The project usually fits one session. If it spans more, save notes after each
  milestone so the next session starts where this one stopped. You only see what the learner pastes: ask
  for the lists and the guide instead of assuming.

## Review
1. The uses are real or planned uses of this group, each with what is at stake if the result
   is wrong.
2. The data rules are concrete, with examples from the group, and based on a look at the
   tool's settings or policy.
3. The checks grow with the stakes, and the guide names when a qualified person must decide.
4. The guide says when AI use is disclosed, and keeps a person in charge where AI touches
   decisions about people.
5. The guide fits on one page, was tested on three situations, changed after a reaction, and
   has a review date.
6. Nothing shared during the project contained personal or confidential information about the
   group or its members.

## Going further
- Share the guide with the whole group and collect the questions it does not answer: they are
  the next version.
- Add a short section on what to do when something goes wrong, and who to tell.
- At the review date, check which rules people actually follow, and simplify the ones they do
  not.
